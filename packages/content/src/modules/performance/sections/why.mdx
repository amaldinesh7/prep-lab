<SectionHeader kind="why" number={1} title="Why performance is product" />

Performance isn't a polish task you do at the end of a quarter. It's a feature you ship every commit, and every commit either pays into it or borrows against it. The reason this matters is brutally simple: slow apps lose money, and they lose it in a way that's almost invisible on the engineering side because no user files a ticket that says "your 800ms LCP cost you a signup." They just leave.

## The numbers everyone quotes for a reason

The studies are old but they keep replicating. Amazon found every 100ms of latency cost them 1% in sales. Google saw a 20% drop in traffic for a half-second increase in search latency. Walmart converted 2% more for every second they cut off load time. Pinterest reduced wait by 40% and got 15% more signups. None of these are about a 5-second page vs a 30-second page — they're about 1.5 vs 2 seconds. The slope of "users tolerating slowness" is steep, and it gets steeper on mobile.

A more recent number: in 2023 Deloitte measured retail sites where a 0.1s mobile speed improvement increased conversion by 8.4%. The unit of slowness isn't seconds anymore. It's hundreds of milliseconds.

The thing to internalize: **users don't have a stopwatch.** They have a feeling. The feeling is "did this thing respond to me?" Every interaction that doesn't get a response within ~100ms feels broken, and the user's confidence in your product erodes. They don't notice it consciously, but they bounce.

## LCP, INP, CLS as the user's stopwatch

Google formalized this with Core Web Vitals (CWV) — three metrics that proxy the three things users actually feel:

- **LCP (Largest Contentful Paint)** — "did the page load?" Measured when the biggest above-the-fold element finishes painting. The user's eye looks for the thing they came for; until it shows up, the page is "not loaded" no matter what your spinner says.
- **INP (Interaction to Next Paint)** — "does it respond to me?" Measured as the worst delay between a user interaction (tap, click, key press) and the next paint that reflects the result. Replaces the old First Input Delay (FID), which only measured the first interaction.
- **CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift)** — "is it stable?" Measured as the total unexpected movement of content during the page's life. The user tries to tap a button, an ad loads, the button jumps, they tap the ad. Maddening.

These aren't arbitrary. Google's research mapped each to user-survey "did this feel fast?" responses and dropped the noise. The thresholds (LCP ≤ 2.5s, INP ≤ 200ms, CLS ≤ 0.1) are calibrated against actual humans, not engineering vibes. And because Google uses them in search ranking, every product team has a business reason to care, not just a UX reason.

## The death by a thousand cuts

Here's the part that catches mid-level React developers: there's almost never one big perf problem in a mature React app. There are forty small ones, and the cumulative cost is what makes the app slow. A single `useEffect` that runs on every render and triggers a fetch isn't a P0 bug. Thirty of them in different components are why your INP is 800ms.

The pattern looks like this in real codebases:

- A 50KB date library shipped to every page because somebody imported it at module top-level
- A 200-row list rendered without virtualization, each row a memo'd component with its own subscribe
- A context provider at the root holding 12 unrelated pieces of state, so every state change rerenders the whole tree
- An analytics script loaded synchronously in `<head>` blocking the main thread for 300ms
- A hero image without `width`/`height` causing a CLS event when fonts load
- A `useEffect` fetch chain four levels deep — child waits for parent waits for layout waits for auth

None of these is "the problem." All of them, together, are why the page feels heavy. The job of a performance-aware engineer is to notice each of them as they're being shipped, not after the user retention dashboard goes red.

<Callout kind="why">
  Performance is the only product feature that erodes silently. Users don't tell you they bounced because the page was 200ms slow — they just bounce. CWV gives you a stopwatch that approximates the user's gut feeling, and the literature has been clear for fifteen years: every 100ms costs revenue. Treat perf as a feature you ship, not a sprint you do once. The compounding cost of "we'll fix it later" is the difference between a product that converts and one that doesn't.
</Callout>
